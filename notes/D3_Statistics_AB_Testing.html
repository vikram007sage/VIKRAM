01 / DESCRIBE

Describing data

Mean vs medianSpend, income and order values are right-skewed: a few huge values pull the mean up. In the D1 card data the mean transaction is ~₹4,000 but the median is ~₹1,760. Report the median for "typical", the mean for totals.
Variance, SDSD = average distance from the mean. Coefficient of variation = SD ÷ mean (compare spread across scales).
Percentilesp50, p90, p99 for latency, spend, loan size. Interquartile range = p75 − p25; outliers often defined as beyond 1.5 × IQR.
CorrelationPearson r measures linear association (−1 to 1). Correlation ≠ causation: ice-cream sales and drownings both rise in summer.
DistributionShape / use
NormalSymmetric; 68–95–99.7% within 1–2–3 SD. Sample means are approximately normal (CLT).
BinomialNumber of successes in n trials (conversions out of visitors). Mean np, variance np(1−p).
PoissonCounts per interval (transactions per minute, fraud alerts per day). Mean = variance = λ.
ExponentialTime between events (time to next purchase).
Log-normal / power lawSpend, income, city sizes, app downloads: long right tails.
02 / INFERENCE

From sample to population

core
Standard error of a mean = s ÷ √n  ·  of a proportion = √(p(1−p) ÷ n)  ·  95% CI = estimate ± 1.96 × SE

Central Limit Theorem

Whatever the shape of the data, the average of a large enough sample is approximately normal, centred on the true mean, with spread s/√n. This is why we can run z- and t-tests on skewed spend data with big samples.

Confidence interval

Card data: mean ₹4,000, SD ₹4,900, n = 616 → 95% CI ≈ ₹3,610 – ₹4,390. Meaning: if we repeated the sampling many times, 95% of such intervals would contain the true mean (not "95% chance the mean is in here").

Margin of error

A survey of 400 people at p = 50% has a margin of error of about ±4.9 points. Quadrupling n halves it.

Hypothesis testing

H₀ / H₁Null: no difference. Alternative: there is one (two-sided unless you have a strong reason).
p-valueIf H₀ were true, the probability of seeing a result at least this extreme. Not the probability H₀ is true, and not the size of the effect.
α (significance)The false-positive rate you accept, usually 5%. Reject H₀ if p < α.
Type I / IIType I = false positive (ship a dud). Type II = false negative (miss a winner). Power = 1 − P(Type II), usually 80%.
Practical vs statisticalWith millions of users, a 0.01% lift can be "significant" and worthless. Always ask whether the effect is big enough to matter.
QuestionTest
Two conversion ratesTwo-proportion z-test (or chi-square)
Two means (spend per user)Welch's t-test (doesn't assume equal variances)
Same users before/afterPaired t-test
3+ groupsANOVA, then pairwise tests with correction
Categorical association / observed vs expected countsChi-square
Very skewed data, small nMann–Whitney U, or bootstrap the difference
03 / EXPERIMENTS

The A/B test workflow, end to end

most asked
1. Hypothesis"Showing why we need PAN on the KYC screen will raise onboarding completion, because users drop when asked for sensitive data without context."
2. MetricsPrimary (one: onboarding completion rate). Secondary (time to complete, day-7 activation). Guardrails (fraud rate, support tickets, app crashes) that must not get worse.
3. Unit + populationRandomise by user (not session) so one person always sees the same variant. Decide who is eligible (new users on Android?).
4. Size itBaseline rate, minimum detectable effect (MDE), α, power → users per arm → duration (section 4). Run at least one full week to cover weekday cycles.
5. Run + checkSample-ratio check on day 1–2, monitor guardrails, don't stop early on a good-looking p-value.
6. AnalyseEffect size, confidence interval, p-value; segments only if planned in advance.
7. DecideShip, iterate or kill, weighing effect size, guardrails, cost and risk. Write it up.

Reading a result (worked)

Control: 1,000 of 10,000 users converted (10.0%). Treatment: 1,100 of 10,000 (11.0%). Pooled two-proportion z = 2.31, p = 0.021. 95% CI for the lift: +0.15 to +1.85 percentage points. Read it as: a 1-point (10% relative) lift, statistically significant at 5%, but the interval is wide — the true lift could be as small as 0.15 points. If shipping is cheap and guardrails are fine, ship; if it's costly, consider a longer test.

04 / SIZING

How many users do you need?

core
Rule of thumb (α = 5%, power = 80%): n per arm ≈ 16 × p(1 − p) ÷ δ²  (δ = absolute MDE)
For means: n per arm ≈ 16 × σ² ÷ δ²
BaselineWant to detectUsers per arm (exact)
10% conversion10% → 11% (+1 pt, 10% relative)≈ 14,750 (rule of thumb: 14,400)
10% conversion10% → 12% (+2 pts)≈ 3,840 (halving δ quadruples n)
Spend per user ₹120, SD ₹400+₹6 (5% lift)≈ 69,800 — revenue metrics are noisy

Underpowered tests

With only 5,000 users per arm, the chance of detecting a true 10% → 11% lift is about 37%. Most "no result" tests at startups are simply too small.

Duration

Days = (n per arm × number of arms) ÷ eligible daily users. Round up to whole weeks; cap at ~4–6 weeks (cookie churn, seasonality).

Choosing the MDE

The smallest lift that would change the decision (pays for the engineering, beats the opportunity cost). Not "whatever we can detect".

Ten ways experiments lie

interview gold
TrapWhat happensFix
PeekingChecking daily and stopping when p < 0.05 inflates false positives far above 5%Fix duration in advance, or use sequential tests (section 6)
Sample-ratio mismatch (SRM)A 50/50 split comes out 10,200 vs 9,800 (chi-square p ≈ 0.005): assignment or logging is brokenCheck SRM first; don't trust any result until fixed

06 / ADVANCED

Techniques big tech uses (know the idea)

CUPED

Use each user's pre-experiment value of the metric as a covariate to remove noise. If the correlation with the pre-period is 0.5, variance falls by 25% (1 − ρ²) — like getting a third more users for free.

Sequential testing

Methods that let you look at results continuously while keeping the false-positive rate honest ("always-valid" p-values, alpha-spending). Common in modern experimentation platforms.

Bayesian A/B

Reports "probability treatment is better" and expected loss instead of a p-value; easier for business audiences, same data. Priors matter for small samples.

Multi-armed bandits

Shift traffic toward the winning variant as you learn. Good for short-lived choices (headlines, offers); worse for learning precise effects.

Quasi-experiments

When you can't randomise: difference-in-differences (compare change in a treated city vs a control city), regression discontinuity (users just above vs below a credit-score cut-off), synthetic control.

Heterogeneous effects

Uplift models estimate who responds to a treatment (who needs a retention offer), not just whether it works on average.

07 / MODELS

Regression and Bayes in two minutes each

Linear regressiony = β₀ + β₁x₁ + … + ε. β₁ = change in y for one unit of x₁, holding the others fixed. R² = share of variance explained (not proof the model is right).
Logistic regressionModels the log-odds of a yes/no outcome (default, churn). exp(β) = odds ratio. The workhorse of credit scorecards.
Assumptions to mentionLinearity, independent errors, constant variance, no strong multicollinearity; check residual plots.
Omitted-variable biasLeaving out a driver that correlates with x biases β. Why observational "users who used feature X spend more" ≠ "feature X causes spend".
Bayes' ruleP(A|B) = P(B|A) P(A) ÷ P(B). Fraud example: 1% of transactions are fraud; the model flags 90% of fraud and 5% of genuine ones. P(fraud | flagged) = 0.9 × 0.01 ÷ (0.9 × 0.01 + 0.05 × 0.99) ≈ 15%. Base rates dominate.
08 / PRACTICE

Fifteen interview questions

answer, then open
1 · Explain a p-value to a non-technical manager.

"If the change really did nothing, how surprising would this result be? A p-value of 0.02 means we'd see a difference this big only 2% of the time by luck. It doesn't tell us how big or valuable the effect is."

2 · Your test shows +3% conversion with p = 0.04 on day 3 of a planned 14-day test. Ship?

No. Early stopping on a good p-value inflates false positives; early days are also skewed by novelty and weekday mix. Finish the planned duration (or use a sequential method designed for early looks), check guardrails and SRM.

3 · Treatment has 52% of users instead of 50%. Does it matter?

Depends on n. With 20,000 users, 10,400 vs 9,600 is a severe SRM (p < 0.001): a bug in assignment, redirects, or logging (e.g. treatment page crashes for some users who then aren't logged). Investigate before reading any metric.

4 · How would you test a new rewards offer for a credit card?

Randomise eligible cardholders; primary metric: spend per active card over 8 weeks; secondary: activation, transactions; guardrails: rewards cost per ₹ spend, delinquency, complaints. Hold out a long-term control to measure whether spend stays after the offer ends. Size on spend variance (noisy), consider CUPED with pre-period spend.

5 · You can't randomise (a regulation applies to all users on one date). How do you measure the impact?

Difference-in-differences against a comparable unaffected group, interrupted time series with seasonality controls, or synthetic control; state the assumptions (parallel trends).

6 · Test wins on mobile and desktop separately but loses overall. How?

Simpson's paradox: treatment got a higher share of the lower-converting segment (or the segments' sizes changed). Check randomisation balance by segment.

7 · A metric moved 2% after a launch. Is it real?

Compare with historical week-to-week variation (what's normal noise?), check for seasonality, data issues, concurrent launches; ideally a holdout. Then decompose (P2 root-cause module).

8 · What's the difference between statistical and practical significance?

Statistical: unlikely to be chance. Practical: big enough to matter for the business. Large samples make tiny effects significant.

9 · How many users for 20% → 22% conversion?

16 × 0.2 × 0.8 ÷ 0.02² = 6,400 per arm (rule of thumb).

10 · Mean or median for average order value? Why?

Report both: median for the typical customer, mean for revenue (it's what multiplies by orders). Skewed data makes the mean sensitive to a few whales; for tests on means, consider capping (winsorising) extreme values.

11 · What is power and why do small companies struggle with it?

The chance of detecting a real effect of a given size. Small user bases mean either long tests or only being able to detect big effects; use bigger changes, CUPED, or more sensitive metrics.

12 · Why randomise by user rather than page view?

So each user has one consistent experience and observations are independent; analysing per page view would overstate sample size and understate variance.

13 · A fraud model flags 2% of transactions. Precision 30%, recall 80%. Explain.

Of flagged transactions, 30% are truly fraud (70% are false alarms that annoy customers). Of all fraud, the model catches 80%. The trade-off is set by the threshold; business cost of each error decides it (M1 module).

14 · Correlation between using the app's budgeting tool and lower delinquency. Should we push the tool to everyone?

Not on that evidence: careful, organised people may both use the tool and repay well (selection). Run a randomised encouragement test (nudge a random half to try it) and compare delinquency.

15 · Walk me through an A/B test you ran.

Your CheQ story (S3 Story 1) in this order: hypothesis, primary metric and guardrails, sample size and duration, result with CI, decision, what you learned. Know every number.
