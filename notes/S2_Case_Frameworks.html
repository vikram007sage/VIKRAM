What a case interview actually tests

01 / THE FLOW

Every case, in seven steps

core
1. Listen + writeWrite the prompt word for word in 4 lines: client, problem, numbers, goal. Note any term you are not 100% sure of ("delinquency rate" — how exactly is it calculated?).
2. Play it back"So, a top-5 Indian card issuer has seen revenue fall 10% over two years and wants to know why and what to do. Is that right?"
3. Clarify (max 3–4 questions)Objective + timeline, the business model, geography/segment, is it only us or the industry? (Section 2 has the list.)
4. Ask for 60 secondsThen draw a tree with 3–4 branches, each with 2–3 sub-points specific to this client. Number them.
5. State a hypothesis"My hunch is the drop is volume-driven, because fees haven't changed. I'd like to start with customer numbers."
6. Drill + do the mathsAsk for data, compute out loud, then say what the number means ("so 80% of the fall is attrition"). Update the hypothesis.
7. SynthesiseAnswer first, then three supporting points, then risks and next steps. 45–60 seconds. Section 13 has the template.

MECE

Mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive. Revenue = price × volume is MECE. "Marketing, competition, customers" is not (they overlap and miss costs). Test: could a cause sit in two branches, or in none?

Hypothesis-driven

Consultants do not analyse everything. They guess the answer early, then try to prove themselves wrong. Say the guess out loud; being wrong is fine, having no guess is not.

So-what

After every number or exhibit, say one line: what it means for the client. "Churn tripled while acquisition is flat, so this is a retention problem, not a marketing one."

03 / FRAMEWORK 1

Profitability: profits are down, why?

most asked
Profit = Revenue − Cost = (Price × Volume) − (Fixed cost + Variable cost per unit × Volume)

Revenue side

Volume = customers × purchases per customer (or market size × share). Price = list price − discounts; check the product mix (shift to cheaper products lowers average price without any price change).

Cost side

Variable: raw material, commissions, payment fees, delivery. Fixed: rent, salaries, tech, depreciation. Ask which line moved, then break that line down along the value chain.

Then go external

Market growth, competitor moves (new entrant, price war), customer shift (UPI replacing cards), regulation (a new RBI rule), macro (rates, inflation).

Worked numbers

A café chain's profit fell from ₹12 cr to ₹6 cr. Revenue: ₹60 cr → ₹63 cr. Costs: ₹48 cr → ₹57 cr. Revenue rose 5%, so the problem is costs (+₹9 cr, +19%). Break costs down: rent ₹10 → 10, staff ₹14 → 15, ingredients ₹18 → 26, other ₹6 → 6. Ingredients explain ₹8 of ₹9 cr. Coffee bean prices rose ~45% → recommend hedging contracts, a menu price rise of ~6% (enough to recover ₹3–4 cr if volume holds), and a smaller cup size option.

Always quantify the split: "Of the ₹6 cr drop, ₹8 cr comes from ingredients, offset by ₹3 cr extra revenue." Interviewers remember candidates who size the driver before proposing fixes.
04 / FRAMEWORK 2

Market entry: should we enter X?

MarketSize (guesstimate it, S1 module), growth, profitability of incumbents, segments, regulation and licences.
CompetitionWho leads, their shares, how they win (price, distribution, brand), how they will react to us. Porter's five forces if the market is unusual.
Client fitCapabilities we can reuse (brand, customers, tech, distribution), what we lack, our right to win.
EconomicsInvestment, unit economics, break-even year, payback, NPV vs alternatives.
Mode + risksBuild vs buy vs partner/JV; key risks and how to test cheaply (pilot city, one segment).
Break-even volume = Fixed costs ÷ (Price − Variable cost per unit)  ·  Payback = Investment ÷ Annual cash flow

Example: a bank considers a gold-loan product. Fixed set-up (branch vaults, staff, tech) ₹40 cr/year. Average loan ₹80,000 at 18% yield, 9% cost of funds, 2% opex, 0.5% credit loss → ₹5,200 contribution per loan per year. Break-even book = 40 cr ÷ 5,200 ≈ 77,000 loans (~₹615 cr book). If the client's branches can source 30 loans/branch/month across 400 branches, that is 1.4 lakh loans a year: enter, starting in the south where gold-loan demand is deepest.

05 / FRAMEWORKS 3–4

Growth (grow revenue 2×) and pricing

Growth: Ansoff grid

Existing products × existing customers: more usage, price, share. New customers: new segments, cities, channels. New products: cross-sell to existing base. Both new: diversify (riskiest). Size each option and rank by size × feasibility × time.

Growth: organic vs inorganic

Organic is slower and cheaper; acquisition is fast but you pay a premium (typically 20–40% over market price) and must integrate. Partnerships (co-branded cards are a classic) sit in between.

Pricing: three lenses

Cost-plus (floor: cost + margin), competitor-based (where alternatives sit), value-based (ceiling: what the customer gains or saves). Price between floor and ceiling; then test elasticity.

Revenue change from a price rise: new revenue = (1 + p)(1 + q) − 1, where q = elasticity × p. A 10% price rise with elasticity −1.5 → (1.10)(0.85) − 1 = −6.5% revenue.
Pricing trap: candidates jump to "charge more". Ask first: who is the customer, what is the alternative, and how price-sensitive are they? A premium travel card can raise its fee; a lifetime-free entry card cannot.
06 / FRAMEWORKS 5–6

M&A / investment and new product launch

Should we buy X?

1) Why: strategic rationale (growth, capability, customers, cost synergies). 2) Target standalone: market, position, financials. 3) Synergies: revenue (cross-sell) and cost (overlap) — realistic, with timing. 4) Price: valuation vs synergies; never pay away all the synergy. 5) Risks: integration, culture, regulation, financing. Exit options if a PE client.

Launch a new product

Customer need and segment size → product and differentiation → go-to-market (channel, price, promotion) → economics (CAC, payback, break-even) → risks and a pilot plan. For tech products, add a metric to watch at launch (activation, D30 retention).

Unconventional cases

No standard framework fits ("Why do so few women use our app?", "Reduce queues at a temple"). Use a customer journey (awareness → consideration → purchase → use → repeat) or a value chain (supply → production → distribution → sale → service) and look for the broken step.

Operations cases

For "reduce waiting time" or "increase output", map the process, find the bottleneck (the slowest step limits the whole line) and use Little's Law.

Little's Law: items in system = arrival rate × time in system. 120 customers/hour × 0.25 hour wait = 30 people in the queue.
07 / INDUSTRY 1

How a credit card business makes money

Amex · Capital One · SBI Card

The players in every swipe

PlayerExampleEarns
CardholderYouRewards, interest-free credit period (up to ~50 days)
IssuerHDFC Bank, SBI Card, ICICI, AxisInterest on revolving balances, fees, most of the interchange
NetworkVisa, Mastercard, RuPay (NPCI), Amex, DinersSmall per-transaction scheme fees; runs the rails and rules
AcquirerBank or payment aggregator that signed up the merchant (Razorpay, Pine Labs)Part of the MDR
MerchantThe shopPays the MDR (≈1.5–2% on most credit cards) for sales and convenience

Amex is "closed loop": it is issuer, network and acquirer at once, so it keeps the full merchant fee — and tends to charge merchants more.

The issuer P&L (memorise this tree)

Profit = Interest income + Fee income + Interchange − Cost of funds − Credit losses − Rewards − Acquisition cost − Opex

Revenue

Interest from revolvers (people who carry a balance; Indian rates are ~36–45% a year). Fees: joining/annual, late payment, forex mark-up, EMI processing, cash advance. Interchange: the issuer's share of the MDR on every swipe.

Costs

Cost of funds (the issuer borrows to fund your credit period). Credit losses (unsecured, so defaults hit hard). Rewards (often 30–50% of interchange). Acquisition (sales commissions, co-brand payouts). Opex (servicing, collections, tech, fraud).

Customer types

Transactors pay in full: low risk, earn interchange + fees only. Revolvers pay interest: most profitable until they default. EMI users sit in between. The mix drives everything.

08 / INDUSTRY 2

How lending works (banks, NBFCs, fintechs)

core
Return on assets ≈ Yield − Cost of funds − Opex − Credit cost + Fees (all as % of loan book)  ·  ROE = ROA × Leverage (assets ÷ equity)
NIMNet interest margin = (interest earned − interest paid) ÷ average earning assets. Banks ~3–4%, cards ~11%.
Cost of fundsBanks: deposits (CASA is cheapest). NBFCs: bank loans, bonds, commercial paper — costlier, so they need higher yields.
Credit costProvisions + write-offs ÷ average loans. Expected loss = PD × LGD × EAD (probability of default × loss given default × exposure at default).
DPD bucketsDays past due: 0 (current), 1–30 (SMA-0), 31–60 (SMA-1), 61–90 (SMA-2), 90+ = NPA. Roll rate = % of a bucket that moves to the next one in a month.
Gross vs net NPAGNPA = NPAs ÷ gross advances; NNPA subtracts provisions. Provision coverage ratio = provisions ÷ GNPA.
Repo linkMost retail floating loans are linked to the RBI repo rate (external benchmark). Repo was cut to 5.25% in December 2025 and raised back to 5.50% on 7 October 2026 with a "calibrated tightening" stance, so loan rates and banks' cost of funds are rising again.

Underwriting: the 5 Cs

Character (bureau score, history), Capacity (income; FOIR = all EMIs ÷ income, usually capped at 50–60%), Capital (own savings), Collateral (LTV = loan ÷ asset value; gold loans capped by RBI), Conditions (purpose, economy).

The loan funnel

Leads → applications → approved → disbursed → repaying → closed. Every funnel case: find the stage where conversion fell. Approval rate and turnaround time (TAT) matter as much as rates.

Collections

Early buckets: reminders, auto-debit retries, digital nudges. Later: calling teams, field agents, settlement, legal (SARFAESI for secured loans). RBI's fair-practice rules limit how and when agents may contact borrowers.

Common interview trap: "approve more loans to grow" without asking about credit quality. Growth bought with loose underwriting shows up as defaults 6–12 months later, the lag that hurt many unsecured lenders in FY25.
09 / PRACTICE

Eight solved cases (try first, then open)

casebook-style
Case 1 · Card issuer revenue down 10% (IIMA-style, profitability)

Prompt: an Indian bank's credit card revenue has fallen 10% over a few years. Only this bank is affected. Assume annual fees are the only revenue.

Structure: Revenue = annual fee × number of cardholders. Fee unchanged → cardholders fell. Cardholders = opening base + new − attrition. Acquisition is steady, attrition up sharply → retention problem. Customer journey: need → awareness → acquisition → usage (before / during / after transaction) → renewal.

Finding: the bank introduced a stricter renewal process (re-submit documents, fresh bureau check) for a compliance requirement that competitors are applying more lightly. Customers find it painful and leave at renewal.

Recommend: short term: early renewal reminders, pre-filled digital renewal, fee waiver for good customers. Long term: build fully online renewal (video KYC, auto bureau pull), check with the regulator what competitors are allowed, and win back churned high-spend customers.

Case 2 · Bank profits down, suspect delinquency (lending, profitability)

First clarify the term: delinquency rate = balances 30+ days past due ÷ total balances. Where is it measured, which products?

Structure: Profit tree → isolate credit cost. Break delinquency by product (cards, personal loans, home), by cohort (month of origination), by channel (branch, digital partner, DSA) and by customer segment.

Data: cards and personal loans sourced through one fintech partner since last year show 30+ DPD of 9% vs 3% for branch-sourced loans; same income bands. The partner's approval rate was 70% vs 40% for branches.

So what: the partner channel loosened underwriting (or has fraud). Recommend: tighten the partner's credit policy (use the bank's own scorecard, cap FOIR), add early-warning triggers, step up collections on the bad cohorts, renegotiate the partner contract with a first-loss guarantee (FLDG, allowed by RBI up to 5% of the portfolio for digital lending).

Case 3 · Home loan book flat for 9 months (funnel)

Prompt: an affordable housing lender (₹10–15 lakh tickets) grew to a ₹1,500 cr book, then stalled.

Structure: Book change = disbursements − repayments − prepayments. Check prepayments first (falling rates trigger balance transfers to banks). Then disbursements = leads × application rate × approval rate × disbursal rate × ticket size.

Finding: leads steady, approval rate down from 60% to 40% after a new centralised credit approval process; TAT rose from 7 to 21 days and customers went to competitors.

Recommend: regional approval authority for standard cases, digital document collection, a TAT target, keep central review only for exceptions.

Case 4 · Should a fintech launch a RuPay-on-UPI credit card? (new product)

Need: young salaried users already pay by UPI QR; a card that works on QR gives them credit + rewards at the same scan.

Economics per user/year (assumed): spend ₹1.2 lakh; interchange ≈0.5% blended (no MDR on small UPI tickets) = ₹600; 25% revolve ₹20,000 avg at 36% → ₹1,800 per revolver, ₹450 per user; fees ₹300. Revenue ≈ ₹1,350. Costs: rewards ₹400, credit loss 4% of ₹15,000 avg balance = ₹600, servicing ₹150, funding (via partner bank) ₹250. Contribution ≈ –₹50 before acquisition: loss-making unless revolve rate or ticket size rises.

Recommend: launch only with a bank partner, target users with existing bureau history, keep rewards on QR modest, push EMI conversion on big purchases, and track 90-day activation and revolve rate as the go/no-go metrics.

Case 5 · Gold-loan market entry for a private bank (entry)

Market: gold loans have been among the fastest-growing secured retail segments as gold prices rose; south India dominates; leaders are specialised NBFCs and large banks.

Right to win: existing branches, lower cost of funds than NBFCs (deposits), existing customers who hold gold.

Gaps: valuation skills, vault security, fast disbursal (NBFCs disburse in ~30 minutes).

Economics: see break-even in section 4. Recommend: enter via 100 southern branches first, train assayers, match 30-minute disbursal, price 1–2 points below NBFCs using the funding advantage; watch LTV discipline (RBI caps it) and gold-price swings.

Case 6 · Reduce cost of a retail bank branch network by 20% (cost)

Structure: Bank cost = interest + provisions + operating cost. Opex = staff + premises + tech + other. Map branch activities: cash, account opening, loans, service requests.

Finding: 60% of branch transactions are routine (passbook, cash deposit, address change) that digital channels can handle; 30% of branches are within 1 km of another.

Recommend: merge overlapping branches, cash recyclers and self-service kiosks, move service requests to the app/WhatsApp banking, redeploy staff to sales. Quantify: closing 15% of branches at ₹1.2 cr opex each saves X; protect deposits by keeping ATMs and relationship managers.

Case 7 · Card spends falling at a premium card (Amex-style)

Structure: Spend = active cards × spend per active card. Active cards = cards in force × activity rate. Spend per card = transactions × ticket size, by category (travel, dining, online, fuel).

Finding: activity steady; travel spend down 30% after a competitor launched a card with better airline miles transfer; customers moved their travel spend, kept everyday spend.

Recommend: new airline/hotel partners, accelerated rewards on travel for 6 months for top-decile spenders, retention calls before annual fee renewal. Measure: share of wallet in travel.

Case 8 · Collections: 30+ DPD rising in a personal-loan book (analytics)

Structure: roll-rate analysis: % moving current → 1–30 → 31–60 → 61–90 → 90+. Find which roll worsened. Segment by vintage, channel, salary-account vs non-salary, ticket size.

Finding: current → 1–30 roll unchanged; 1–30 → 31–60 roll doubled after the bank cut its early-bucket calling team to save cost.

Recommend: restore early-bucket contact (cheapest bucket to cure), add UPI AutoPay/e-mandate retries on salary day, use a propensity-to-pay model to prioritise calls. Show the maths: each 1% of book cured early saves ~LGD × 1% of book in losses.

10 / MATHS

The numbers that come up in every case

GrowthCAGR = (End ÷ Start)^(1/years) − 1. Rule of 72: doubling time ≈ 72 ÷ growth %.
MarginsGross margin = (Revenue − COGS) ÷ Revenue; contribution margin = (Price − Variable cost) ÷ Price.
Unit economicsLTV = monthly margin per user × average lifetime (months) = margin ÷ monthly churn. Healthy: LTV ÷ CAC ≥ 3, CAC payback < 12–18 months.
NPVNPV = Σ CFt ÷ (1 + r)t − Investment. Perpetuity = CF ÷ r; growing perpetuity = CF ÷ (r − g).
Market shareRelative share = our share ÷ largest competitor's share.
Percent vs pointsNPA rising from 2% to 3% is +1 percentage point, or +50%. Say which one you mean.
Drill: a card costs ₹2,500 to acquire and earns ₹150 a month; 2% of customers leave each month. Worth it?

Lifetime = 1 ÷ 0.02 = 50 months; LTV = 150 × 50 = ₹7,500 (undiscounted). LTV/CAC = 3.0: borderline healthy. Payback = 2,500 ÷ 150 ≈ 17 months.

Drill: a ₹1,000 cr loan book, PD 3%, LGD 60%. Expected loss?

EL = 1,000 × 0.03 × 0.60 = ₹18 cr, i.e. 1.8% credit cost. If yield 14%, cost of funds 7.5%, opex 2.5%, ROA ≈ 14 − 7.5 − 2.5 − 1.8 = 2.2% before tax.

11 / CLOSE

Ending the case well

core

Template (45–60 s): "Recommendation: the client should X. Three reasons: first..., second..., third... (each with a number). Risks: the main risk is Y, which we'd mitigate by Z. Next steps: validate A with B data in the next two weeks."

Do

Answer first. Use the numbers you computed. Commit to one recommendation. Mention one risk. Stand up (literally, in a virtual interview, look at the camera).

Don't

Recap your whole analysis in order. Say "it depends". Add a new idea you never tested. List seven recommendations.

If you got stuck

Say so calmly: "Let me step back to the objective." Re-read your tree, pick the next branch, and state a new hypothesis. Recovering well scores better than never stumbling.
