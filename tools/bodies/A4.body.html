03 / INTERVIEW FAVOURITE

Bayes, done the easy way

asked a lot

Skip the formula; imagine 10,000 people and count.

A disease affects 1% of people. The test catches 99% of cases but also flags 5% of healthy people. You test positive. Probability you're ill?

  1. 10,000 people → 100 ill, 9,900 healthy.
  2. Ill and positive: 99% of 100 = 99.
  3. Healthy but positive: 5% of 9,900 = 495.
  4. P(ill | positive) = 99 / (99 + 495) = 99/594 = 1/6 ≈ 16.7%.

Why it's low: the disease is rare, so false positives from the huge healthy group swamp the true positives. Card-fraud models face exactly this problem, which is why analyst interviews ask it.

04 / TRADER CORE

Expected value and pricing games

Linearity of expectation

E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y], even when X and Y are dependent. It turns hard problems into sums.

Expected sum of 3 dice = 3 × 3.5 = 10.5. Expected number of people who get their own hat back when hats are shuffled = n × (1/n) = 1.

Price a game with a reroll

Roll a die and receive the face value in rupees. You may reroll once, but then you must keep the second roll. Fair price?

  1. A second roll is worth 3.5.
  2. Keep a first roll of 4, 5 or 6 (better than 3.5); reroll 1, 2, 3.
  3. EV = ½ × 5 (avg of 4,5,6) + ½ × 3.5 = 4.25.

Variance

Var(X) = E[X²] − (E[X])²

One die: 91/6 − 3.5² = 35/12 ≈ 2.92. Traders care because a positive-EV bet with huge variance can still ruin you.

How to answer out loud

  1. Define the outcomes and their probabilities.
  2. Write EV as a sum.
  3. Check the edge cases.
  4. State the decision: "I'd pay up to ₹4.25."
05 / PUZZLES

Fifteen classic puzzles

say the reasoning, not just the answer
1. Monty Hall: switch or stay?

Switch: you win 2/3 of the time. Your first pick is right 1/3 of the time. The host always opens an empty door from the other two, so the remaining closed door carries the other 2/3.

2. A family has two children, at least one is a boy. P(both boys)?

1/3. Equally likely families: BB, BG, GB, GG. "At least one boy" removes GG, leaving 3, of which 1 is BB. (If told the older child is a boy, it's 1/2.)

3. How many people before two share a birthday with probability over 50%?

23 (≈ 50.7%). There are 253 pairs among 23 people; that's why it's so low.

4. Expected tosses of a fair coin until HH? Until HT?

HH: 6. HT: 4. After an H, a T sends HH back to the start, but for HT a second H keeps you one step away. That asymmetry is the whole puzzle.

5. Expected rolls until you've seen all six faces?

14.7. Coupon collector: 6 × (1 + 1/2 + … + 1/6) = 6 × 2.45.

6. Break a stick at two random points. P(the three pieces form a triangle)?

1/4. A triangle needs every piece shorter than half the stick; that region is a quarter of the possible break points.

7. Three ants on the corners of a triangle each walk along a random edge. P(no collision)?

No collision only if all go clockwise or all anticlockwise: 2 of 8 → P(no collision) = 1/4, so P(collision) = 3/4.

8. 25 horses, 5 tracks, no stopwatch. Fewest races to find the fastest 3?

7. 5 heats; race the 5 winners (6th race) → the overall fastest is known. Only 5 horses can still be 2nd or 3rd (2nd and 3rd of the winner's heat, 1st and 2nd of the runner-up's heat, the winner of the 3rd-place heat): race them (7th).

9. 12 coins, one is heavier or lighter. A balance scale. Fewest weighings?

3. Each weighing has 3 outcomes, so 3 weighings distinguish 27 cases; you need 24 (12 coins × heavy/light). Start with 4 vs 4.

10. Two ropes each burn in exactly 60 minutes, unevenly. Measure 45 minutes.

Light rope A at both ends and rope B at one end. A burns out in 30 minutes; then light B's other end. B finishes 15 minutes later: 45.

11. Four people cross a bridge at night with one torch (1, 2, 5, 10 minutes). Fastest total?

17 minutes. 1+2 cross (2), 1 returns (1), 5+10 cross (10), 2 returns (2), 1+2 cross (2).

12. Jugs of 3 L and 5 L. Measure exactly 4 L.

Fill 5 → pour into 3 (2 L left) → empty 3 → pour the 2 L in → fill 5 → top up the 3 (uses 1 L) → 4 L left in the 5 L jug.

13. Two eggs, 100 floors. Fewest drops to find the highest safe floor, worst case?

14. Drop from 14, then 27, 39, … (each step one floor shorter) so that the total worst case stays 14, because 14 + 13 + … + 1 = 105 ≥ 100.

14. 100 lockers, 100 students; student k toggles every k-th locker. How many stay open?

10. A locker is toggled once per divisor; only perfect squares have an odd number of divisors (1, 4, 9, …, 100).

15. How many times do a clock's hands overlap in 24 hours?

22. Once every 65 5/11 minutes: 11 times every 12 hours.
